Federated Edge-Based Intrusion Detection and Local Mitigation for Smart City IoT Systems: A Detection-to-Response Latency Evaluation


Federated Edge-Based Intrusion Detection - is about our system, which takes GRU attention model, trained by Federated Avg on several edge nodes


 Local Mitigation - after the model detects the attack, the action is taken on the same node. In most papers, action is taken by a centralized SDN controller.
 
Detection-to-Response Latency Evaluation - we measure the full time from the arrival of a flow to the moment the action is finished

INTRODUCTION


Cities put more and more sensors into their utility systems. Water meters, heat meters, street lights and building controls are now networked devices spread across a city and run by utility operators. These devices bring real benefits, but they also open the city up to attack. There are many of them, they sit in places people can reach, they are rarely updated, and each one is too small to run normal security software. If an attacker succeeds, the problem is not only stolen data. A service that residents depend on can stop working.
The usual answer is a central intrusion detection system. Traffic is sent from the field to a central server, the server classifies it, and an alert is raised. This design has three known problems in a city-wide network. First, every decision waits for the traffic to travel to the centre, and the response has to travel back, so the delay is paid twice. Second, sending raw traffic all the time uses up uplink bandwidth, and in a utility network those flows can show when a building is occupied or how much it consumes, which raises privacy concerns. Third, the central server is a single point of failure for the whole system.
Two lines of research deal with these problems. The first one moves detection to the edge of the network. Recent work shows that detection models can run on gateway-class hardware, but the speed depends strongly on the model. A lightweight two-stage detector needs 26.66 microseconds per flow on a Raspberry Pi 3 [8]. A quantized temporal convolutional network needs 0.19 ms on a Raspberry Pi 4 [10]. A tuned tree ensemble needs 4.3 ms on a Raspberry Pi 5 [9], and an adaptive online model needs 34.8 ms on the same class of device [11]. At the architecture level, a micro-edge design reports about 50 ms detection latency, against about 200 ms for a gateway and 500 ms for the cloud on similar smart city traffic [7]. These gains are not automatic. A hybrid TinyML detector on the same kind of hardware needs 120-180 ms per sample, and its authors say this is too slow for strict real-time use [12]. So the speed comes from the pair of model and device, not from the word "edge".
The second line removes the need to send raw traffic at all. Federated learning trains one shared model across many nodes by exchanging model parameters instead of data [22]. The common finding is that this costs very little accuracy. One method reaches 94.5% accuracy on Edge-IIoTset and 99.2% on CIC-IoT2023, and reports a federated-to-centralized comparison of 92.49% against 93.92% on the first dataset [1]. A systematic benchmark of FedAvg, FedProx and FedNova on Edge-IIoTset, CIC-IoT2023 and TII-SSRC-23 reports F1 of 93.0% for FedAvg and 94.0% for FedProx on Edge-IIoTset, and shows that FedNova reaches the target accuracy in about a third of the rounds while sending 15-25% less data [2]. Similar results appear on ToN_IoT, CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 [3]. In smart city settings, a federated detector reaches 98.51% accuracy with a 1.49% miss rate while keeping feature selection decentralized [19], and federated learning has been compared with split learning on urban IoT attack data [20]. The open question in this line is data heterogeneity: when clients see different data, the choice of aggregation rule stops being neutral [2].
Work that combines both ideas already exists, and we state this clearly. HED-ID trains a stacked BiGRU with temporal and spatial attention using federated averaging across ten non-IID clients, runs it on constrained hardware, and reports 95.6% accuracy on a server, 93.8% on a Raspberry Pi 4, and 18-22 ms inference latency [6]. FLEX-IDS adds explanations and adversarial robustness in an Edge-of-Things setting [5], and federated training at IoT edge nodes has been studied on its own [4]. The attention part is also well established. Attention-based GRU models reach F1 98.94% on BoT-IoT [13] and F1 0.946 on Edge-IIoTset [14], and one study reports 99% accuracy on ToN-IoT with a classification time of 0.075-0.3 ms, against 0.489-0.55 ms for a plain GRU [15]. For this reason we do not claim that combining federated learning, attention and edge deployment is new.
What gets much less attention is the step after detection. In the edge papers [6]-[12], the pipeline ends at a decision: latency is measured up to the alert, and no action follows. In the federated papers [1]-[5], detection quality is measured but latency is usually not reported at all. Where automatic response does exist, it is placed centrally. Federated reinforcement learning produces mitigation policies that run on SDN controllers, with 95.1% detection accuracy across twenty controllers [17]. Collaborative federated defense blocks attacks through cooperation between autonomous systems [16]. A recent review shows that the standard pattern in software-defined networks is for the controller to block a port and unblock it later [18]. In all of these, the action is taken by the same kind of central element that edge deployment was meant to avoid. Outside intrusion detection, researchers have also argued that detection latency is an under-reported metric [23].
As a result, few studies measure the complete path from attack traffic, through detection at the edge and a security decision, to an action that is actually applied, and report how long that path takes. This paper looks at that path. We propose a federated edge-based intrusion detection system. A GRU-Attention model is trained across simulated edge nodes with federated averaging, runs locally on each node, and is connected to a simple three-level policy: allow, quarantine, block. The action is applied on the node that made the detection. No raw traffic leaves the node, and no alert is sent to a central component before the action is taken. We evaluate the system on Edge-IIoTset [21] with both IID and non-IID client splits.
We make four contributions. First, we describe an architecture where federated training, local detection and local mitigation are closed on a single simulated edge node, and we give the rule that turns a model output into an action. Second, we compare centralized and federated training of the same GRU-Attention model under IID and non-IID splits. We treat this as a check before edge deployment, not as a contribution of its own. Third, we define and measure a latency framework that separates detection latency, mitigation latency and end-to-end detection-to-response latency. Fourth, we compare local response with an emulated central response under the same network conditions, which separates the effect of where the response happens from the effect of how the model was trained.
The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 states the gap and our contributions. Section 4 presents the architecture. Section 5 describes the federated IDS and Section 6 the mitigation mechanism. Section 7 gives the experimental setup, Section 8 the results and Section 9 the discussion. Section 10 lists the limitations and Section 11 concludes.
